\begin{abstract}
Partial volume effects (PVE) systematically bias quantitative SPECT and PET, especially for small or irregular lesions whose measured uptake may fall below clinical decision thresholds.
Standard recovery-coefficient (RC) corrections are calibrated on spherical inserts and do not transfer to the stochastic morphologies of real lesions.
We present a proof-of-concept framework that uses radiolabeled tissue-equivalent gelatin to generate a large library of irregular, contrast-visible segments with known volume and activity density.
Across six experimental sessions we acquired 75 SPECT/CT scans of 21 phantom containers, yielding more than two thousand validated segments spanning $0.12$--$80.07\,\mu\mathrm{Ci/ml}$.
Computed tomography (CT) provides morphological ground truth, while nuclear-medicine (NM) images supply PVE-affected observations; radiomic features extracted from the NM data are then mapped to the ground-truth targets with machine-learning models.
On a held-out phantom session, a linear-regression baseline on mean intensity with a random-forest residual correction recovered activity density with $R^2=0.89$, and an elastic-net model recovered volume with $R^2=0.76$, both outperforming a classical NEMA RC curve on small and irregular segments.
The construction is isotope-agnostic: $^{99m}\mathrm{Tc}$ is used here as a convenient model tracer, but the same protocol extends to therapeutic isotopes such as $^{177}\mathrm{Lu}$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Nuclear medicine is currently undergoing a transformative paradigm shift, transitioning from a primarily qualitative diagnostic tool to a robust quantitative imaging science.
In the contemporary era of personalized oncology and theranostics, the ability to accurately measure absolute radiotracer uptake is no longer an auxiliary feature but a clinical imperative~\citep{soret2007partial,erlandsson2012review}.
High-stakes therapeutic decisions, such as determining patient eligibility for targeted radionuclide therapies like $^{177}\mathrm{Lu}$-PSMA, are increasingly governed by specific standardized uptake value (SUV) thresholds derived from PET imaging~\citep{jentzen2011phantom}.
Furthermore, the rising importance of internal dosimetry and the utilization of bone scintigraphy as a quantitative biomarker demand a level of precision that transcends traditional visual interpretation~\citep{zeintl2010quantitative,seret2012quantitative}.

However, the clinical reliability of these quantitative metrics is fundamentally undermined by the partial volume effect (PVE).
PVE is a pervasive phenomenon in emission tomography---affecting both positron emission tomography (PET) and single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT)---that leads to a significant degradation of signal fidelity~\citep{soret2007partial,erlandsson2012review,aston2002positron}.
The clinical consequence of PVE is most severe in small lesions or regions characterized by high activity gradients, where it results in a systematic underestimation of activity concentration~\citep{hoffman1979quantitation,kessler1984analysis}.
When a small malignant focus is subject to these effects, its measured SUV may fall below critical clinical cut-off points, potentially leading to false-negative diagnoses or the denial of life-saving treatments to eligible patients.

Technically, PVE is driven by the finite spatial resolution of imaging systems and is commonly described through two primary mechanisms.
The first is the system's point-spread function (PSF), which causes a ``spill-over'' (cross-contamination) of counts between adjacent regions~\citep{aston2002positron,rousset1998correction,frouin2002correction}.
This manifests as spill-out from the target lesion into the background and spill-in from surrounding tissues into the region of interest.
The second mechanism is the tissue fraction effect, where a single voxel encapsulates a mixture of tissues with varying uptake levels, further blurring the boundaries and complicating accurate quantification~\citep{meltzer1990correction,mullergartner1992measurement}.

To mitigate these inaccuracies, various correction strategies have been proposed over the past decades, including recovery coefficient (RC) methods, deconvolution-based algorithms, and structural image-guided approaches~\citep{rousset1998correction,baete2004evaluation}.
While showing promise, many of these techniques suffer from high sensitivity to noise or a heavy reliance on perfect anatomical segmentation~\citep{erlandsson2012review,hoetjes2010partial}.
Historically, validation studies have utilized phantoms containing a limited number of spherical inserts of known sizes to calculate RCs~\citep{hoffman1979quantitation,geworski2000recovery}.
However, more recent research has demonstrated that geometry plays a decisive role; for instance, circular and elliptical structures of identical volumes exhibit disparate recovery coefficients~\citep{zeintl2010quantitative,gallivanone2011pve}.
This implies that clinical lesions---which are inherently irregular and stochastic in shape---possess unpredictable RCs that cannot be accurately corrected using idealized, rigid geometric models~\citep{jentzen2011phantom,seret2012quantitative}.

In this study, we present a proof of concept for a novel PVE validation framework based on radiolabeled tissue-equivalent gelatinous matrices.
Unlike traditional ``perfect'' geometric phantoms, the unique physical properties of this medium allow for the efficient generation of a vast repository of stochastic, asymmetrical, and morphologically heterogeneous segments.
By leveraging this method, we successfully produced a high-density dataset of over $3{,}000$ unique segments, each with precisely calibrated volumes and activity densities.

This ``big data'' environment allows for an advanced analysis of PVE that was previously unattainable with sparse phantom data.
Because the irregular geometry of these segments causes traditional mathematical recovery models to collapse, we employed radiomics to capture a high-dimensional feature set, including complex spatial and textural patterns that are often ``smoothed'' by PVE.
These features were then integrated into a machine-learning (ML) framework to bridge the statistical gap between image-derived values and ground-truth metrics.
This synergy of morphological realism and predictive modeling aims to provide a robust, data-driven PVE correction that is uniquely adaptable to the intricate irregularities of real-world clinical findings~\citep{salmon2018quantitative,trangia2019characterization,southekal2012evaluation,matsubara2020model,denijs2014improving}.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{General algorithm and experimental rationale}

The core objective of this methodology is to provide a data-driven solution to PVE by creating a high-throughput ``data factory'' for SPECT/CT analysis.
The algorithm is based on a four-stage pipeline designed to bridge the gap between idealized phantoms and clinical reality:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Contrast-based ground truth.}
We developed a two-phase phantom system where the background matrix consists of non-active gelatin, and the target segments consist of gelatin radiolabeled with $^{99m}\mathrm{Tc}$ and enriched with an iodinated CT contrast agent.
This design allows the CT scan to serve as the absolute morphological ground truth, providing precise volume and shape data, while the SPECT scan captures the distorted functional data affected by PVE.

\item \textbf{Stochastic data augmentation.}
To ensure statistical robustness, multiple containers were utilized.
By physically agitating (shaking) the containers between scans and utilizing the natural decay of the isotope across multiple experimental sessions, we generated a ``big data'' environment of over $2{,}000$ unique, non-idealized segments from a single experimental framework.

\item \textbf{Proof of concept and scalability.}
$^{99m}\mathrm{Tc}$ was strategically selected as a model isotope due to its availability and convenient half-life, enabling the rapid generation of a massive dataset.
This establishes a cost-effective methodology that is fundamentally generalizable to more expensive or logistically complex isotopes, such as $^{177}\mathrm{Lu}$ or various PET tracers.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Phantom preparation and formulation}

To maintain physical consistency and ensure that the only variables were activity and contrast, a standardized preparation protocol was followed:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Base gelatin matrix.}
Distilled water was heated to a constant $65^\circ\mathrm{C}$ using a precision heating bath.
Gelatin powder was integrated at a ratio of $19\,\mathrm{g/L}$ and stirred for approximately one hour until a uniform, fully melted distribution was achieved.
This identical base matrix was used for both the background and the active segments to ensure matching attenuation and density properties.

\item \textbf{Active segment formulation.}
For the target segments, a dark-pigmented juice was used as a solvent to aid visual identification during preparation (\Cref{fig:phantom}, right).
An iodinated contrast agent ($10\,\mathrm{mL/L}$) was added to ensure high-contrast visibility and facilitate automated, high-precision segmentation in the CT scan (\Cref{fig:phantom}, left).

\item \textbf{Radiolabeling.}
Radioactive $^{99m}\mathrm{Tc}$ was integrated into these segments at varying activity densities across different sessions.
These segments were then randomly distributed within the non-active background gelatin inside multiple containers.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Data volume and acquisition density}

The high-throughput nature of the data factory is summarized in \Cref{tab:acquisition}.
Data were collected over six extensive experimental sessions, generating a total of $2{,}302$ validated segments.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Dynamic range.}
The activity density across the sessions spanned from $0.12$ to $80.07\,\mu\mathrm{Ci/ml}$, providing the models with a vast range of signal intensities to learn from.

\item \textbf{Acquisition density.}
A total of $75$ scans were performed across $21$ unique phantom containers.
Between scans, the phantoms were physically agitated to redistribute the segments, and scans were repeated over time to utilize natural radioactive decay, thereby increasing the diversity of the feature space.

\item \textbf{Test dataset integrity.}
Consistent with our validation strategy, the final session (session~$0$), containing $334$ segments, was held out entirely for independent testing (``new phantom'' simulation).
This test set was characterized by unique activity ranges ($3.6$--$10.2\,\mu\mathrm{Ci/ml}$), ensuring that the model's accuracy was not biased by the training distribution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Imaging protocol and reconstruction}

Data acquisition was performed using a Siemens Symbia Pro.specta SPECT/CT scanner at Shaare Zedek Medical Center.
The SPECT protocol consisted of $360^\circ$ projections with an angular dwell time of $3$ seconds per degree.
Images were reconstructed using the ordered subset expectation maximization (OSEM) algorithm with attenuation correction (AC), $48$ iterations, and $1$ subset.
A clinical-standard 3D Gaussian filter ($8\,\mathrm{mm}$ FWHM) was applied to the final volumes to simulate standard clinical resolution.

\subsection{Automated segmentation and data integrity}

A dedicated multi-stage pipeline was developed to match functional and anatomical data (\Cref{fig:segmentation}):

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{CT segmentation (ground truth).}
Anatomical boundaries were defined using a fixed threshold of $60\,\mathrm{HU}$.

\item \textbf{NM segmentation.}
Functional volumes were initially thresholded at $8\%$ of the global maximum, followed by a local refinement at $40\%$ of the segment's peak voxel value.

\item \textbf{Recursive refinement.}
If a refined segment's volume was $<60\%$ of its initial estimate, the algorithm recursively searched for a secondary local maximum ($>70\%$ of the primary peak) and reapplied the refinement.
This iterative approach ensures that the segments remain representative of irregular, non-spherical clinical morphologies.

\item \textbf{Data cleaning.}
To ensure a unique correspondence between the ``truth'' and the ``observation,'' any segment without a strict $1$:$1$ CT-to-NM match (e.g., overlapping segments or merged clusters) was excluded from the final dataset.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Radiomics and machine learning architecture}

The final dataset, comprising over $3{,}000$ validated segments, was utilized to develop and train predictive models for PVE correction.
The architecture was structured as follows.

\paragraph{Feature extraction and selection.}
High-dimensional radiomic features were extracted from the NM data using a fixed bin width of $2{,}000$ counts.
This standardization ensured a constant intensity resolution across varying activity levels and scan sessions.
To prevent overfitting and reduce collinearity, features with near-zero variance and those exhibiting high mutual correlation were excluded.

\paragraph{Predictive modeling strategy.}
We implemented two distinct algorithmic approaches based on the target variable:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Activity density estimation.}
A random forest (RF) model was selected to capture the complex, non-linear relationship between textural radiomic features and PVE distortions.
To further enhance robustness, a hybrid model (LR$+$RF) was developed, where a linear regression (LR) baseline was refined by an RF-based error correction.

\item \textbf{Volume quantification.}
Elastic net (EN) was utilized as a regularized linear model.
While RF showed high accuracy in internal testing, EN was prioritized for its superior stability in recovering morphological volume across different phantom geometries.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Clinical simulation and validation (hold-out).}
To ensure the model's generalizability to real-world clinical data, two validation protocols were employed:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Standard randomized split.}
A conventional $80/20$ train--test split to measure internal consistency.

\item \textbf{Phantom-based ``new patient'' simulation.}
The model was tested on an entirely unseen phantom session.
Given the stochastic nature of each phantom (e.g., variations in segment clustering and manual agitation patterns), this protocol validates the system's ability to generalize to novel spatial distributions rather than memorizing session-specific artifacts.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Benchmark comparison (classic RC model).}
As a clinical baseline, a standard recovery coefficient (RC) curve was established using a NEMA image quality (IQ) phantom ($4.1\,\mu\mathrm{Ci/ml}$ spheres, zero background).
Following the methodology in MIRD Pamphlet No.~32~\citep{mird32}, the RC data were fitted to a sigmoidal empirical model
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rc}
\mathrm{RC}(V) = 1 - \frac{1}{1 + (V/a)^{b}},
\end{equation}
where $V$ is the volume and $a,b$ are fitted parameters.
This ``idealized'' curve was compared against the ML models to demonstrate the improvement in quantitative accuracy, particularly for small-volume and irregularly shaped clinical targets.

\section{Results}

Segments that lacked a unique CT--NM correspondence (one CT segment matched to more than one NM segment, or vice versa) were filtered out of the dataset.
Features with low variance and features with high mutual correlation were then removed.
A random forest was trained to estimate segment activity density and segment volume, and evaluated on the held-out test data.

On a randomized train--test split, the activity-density RF model attained $R^2=0.98$ and $\mathrm{RMSE}=0.48\,\mu\mathrm{Ci/ml}$.
To probe constancy under a distribution shift, the same model was evaluated on a new phantom rather than a random split of the pooled dataset.
In this case $R^2$ fell to $0.86$ with $\mathrm{RMSE}=0.85\,\mu\mathrm{Ci/ml}$.
To improve these results, a linear regression on mean intensity counts was used as a first-stage predictor and the RF was trained to correct the residual.
The combined model improved the new-phantom scores to $R^2=0.89$ and $\mathrm{RMSE}=0.75\,\mu\mathrm{Ci/ml}$.

For volume, an elastic-net model was compared with RF under both the random split and the new-phantom protocol.
Although the random-split $R^2$ of EN is only slightly higher than its new-phantom $R^2$, the difference is insignificant, indicating that the volume mapping is stable across phantom geometries.
RF, while stronger than EN on the random split, performed poorly on the new phantom, leading to the conclusion that RF does not generalize for volume prediction.

Relative to a classic recovery-coefficient curve fitted on an image-quality phantom, the RF activity-density model is substantially more accurate at small volumes, where the spherical RC model fails.
A residual train--test discrepancy remains for the RF model, but the difference is insignificant relative to the RC baseline.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presented a method for estimating the activity density and the volume of a lesion using machine-learning algorithms on radiomic properties of approximately $2{,}000$ lesions which, unlike previous methods, include irregular shapes rather than spheres.
For activity-density estimation, the best results were obtained by linear regression on the NM intensity measurement with random-forest residual correction, with $R^2=0.89$ on a new phantom.
For volume estimation, $R^2=0.76$ was obtained with an elastic-net model.
