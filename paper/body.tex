\begin{abstract}
Partial volume effects (PVE) systematically bias quantitative SPECT, especially for small or irregular lesions.
Standard recovery-coefficient (RC) corrections are calibrated on spherical inserts and do not transfer to the stochastic morphologies of real lesions.
We present a proof-of-concept framework that uses radiolabeled gelatin to generate a library of irregular, contrast-visible inserts.
Computed tomography (CT) supplies a high-resolution morphological reference (volume and shape); activity density is set by the labeled gelatin preparation; SPECT supplies the PVE-affected observation.
Across six experimental sessions we acquired $75$ SPECT/CT scans of $21$ phantom containers, yielding $2{,}302$ validated one-to-one CT--SPECT segment pairs in a cold (non-radioactive) gelatin background.
Activity density spanned $22.2$--$436.6\,\mathrm{kBq/ml}$.
One session ($334$ segments) was held out as a new-preparation test.
The protocol is demonstrated with $^{99m}\mathrm{Tc}$ as a model tracer; the same construction is transferable in spirit to therapeutic isotopes such as $^{177}\mathrm{Lu}$.
Radiomic features extracted from SPECT were mapped to the reference targets with machine-learning models.
On the held-out session, a linear-regression baseline on mean intensity with a random-forest residual correction recovered activity density with $R^2=0.89$, and an elastic-net model recovered volume with $R^2=0.76$, both outperforming a classical NEMA RC curve on small and irregular segments.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Quantitative SPECT is increasingly used to measure absolute radiotracer uptake for internal dosimetry and for activity-concentration imaging~\citep{erlandsson2012review,zeintl2010quantitative,seret2012quantitative}.
The better-known clinical analogue is PET, where standardized uptake value (SUV) thresholds can govern eligibility for targeted radionuclide therapy~\citep{soret2007partial}.
In both modalities, the quantity that matters is activity concentration, not visual intensity.

That quantity is biased by the partial volume effect (PVE).
Finite spatial resolution, described by the system's point-spread function (PSF), spills counts out of a hot lesion into the background and into the lesion from surrounding tissue~\citep{soret2007partial,erlandsson2012review,aston2002positron,hoffman1979quantitation,kessler1984analysis}.
A second contribution is the tissue-fraction effect: a voxel on an edge mixes lesion and background.
The result is a systematic underestimation of activity concentration in small lesions and in regions with steep activity gradients.

Recovery-coefficient (RC) corrections remain the usual practical response.
They are almost always calibrated on a small set of spherical inserts of known size~\citep{hoffman1979quantitation,geworski2000recovery,jentzen2011phantom}.
Geometry changes the result: circular and elliptical objects of equal volume already have different RCs~\citep{zeintl2010quantitative,gallivanone2011pve}.
RC is therefore not a function of volume alone, and a sphere-calibrated curve is a poor model for irregular clinical lesions.
Deconvolution and anatomy-guided partial-volume methods exist, but they are sensitive to noise and to errors in the anatomical segmentation~\citep{erlandsson2012review,hoetjes2010partial,rousset1998correction,baete2004evaluation}.
Irregular physical phantoms (anthropomorphic or 3D-printed) can test shape dependence, yet they are costly to produce at the scale needed for empirical, data-driven correction.

This paper is a proof of concept for a gelatin phantom that yields many irregular, CT-visible inserts from one preparation, imaged with SPECT/CT.
The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a two-compartment gelatin construction in which iodinated, $^{99m}\mathrm{Tc}$-labeled inserts sit in a cold gelatin background, so that CT provides a high-resolution morphological reference while SPECT records the PVE-affected observation;
\item a SPECT/CT library of $2{,}302$ validated one-to-one CT--SPECT segment pairs from $75$ scans of $21$ containers over six sessions, with one session held out as a new-preparation test;
\item a data-driven mapping from SPECT radiomics to CT volume and prepared activity density, later compared with a spherical NEMA RC curve.
\end{enumerate}
The study is deliberately narrow: cold background only, overlapping or merged inserts excluded, $^{99m}\mathrm{Tc}$ SPECT rather than a patient partial-volume correction or a $^{177}\mathrm{Lu}$/PET demonstration.
The construction is transferable in spirit to other isotopes; it is demonstrated here only with $^{99m}\mathrm{Tc}$.

The experiment follows the pipeline in \Cref{fig:pipeline}: gelatin inserts are prepared and imaged with SPECT/CT; containers are shaken and rescanned, including at later times as the tracer decays; CT and SPECT segments are paired only when the match is one-to-one; radiomic features from SPECT are then mapped to the reference volume and activity density.

\subsection{Phantom preparation}

Distilled water was heated to a constant $65^\circ\mathrm{C}$.
Gelatin powder was added at $19\,\mathrm{g/L}$ and stirred for approximately one hour until fully melted.
This base matrix, which is approximately water-equivalent, was used for both the background and the labeled inserts.

For the inserts, a dark-pigmented juice was used as a solvent to aid visual identification during preparation (\Cref{fig:phantom}, right).
An iodinated contrast agent ($10\,\mathrm{mL/L}$) was added so that inserts are visible on CT and can be segmented automatically (\Cref{fig:phantom}, left).
$^{99m}\mathrm{Tc}$ was mixed into the insert gelatin at a chosen activity density in each session.
The labeled fragments were then distributed in non-radioactive (cold) background gelatin inside multiple containers.

Iodine is added only to the inserts, so their X-ray attenuation is not matched to the background.
The same CT is used for attenuation correction and for morphological segmentation; any bias this contrast difference introduces is not measured here (\Cref{sec:discussion}).

Between scans, containers were physically agitated.
That step resamples insert pose and clustering; it does not create new independent lesions.
Scans were also repeated over time so that radioactive decay varies count level and noise while morphology is unchanged.

\subsection{SPECT/CT acquisition and reconstruction}

Data were acquired on a Siemens Symbia Pro.specta SPECT/CT scanner at Shaare Zedek Medical Center.
The SPECT protocol used $360^\circ$ projections with an angular dwell time of $3$ seconds per degree.
Images were reconstructed with ordered subset expectation maximization (OSEM), attenuation correction (AC), $48$ iterations, and $1$ subset.
A $3$D Gaussian filter ($8\,\mathrm{mm}$ FWHM) was applied to the reconstructed volumes.
The same CT acquisition was used for attenuation correction and for the morphological mask.

\subsection{Segmentation, matching, and exclusions}

CT and SPECT volumes were segmented and paired as in \Cref{fig:segmentation}.

\paragraph{CT (morphological reference).}
Insert boundaries were defined by a fixed threshold of $60\,\mathrm{HU}$, chosen as the most promising cut in a small-scale experiment.
Reported CT volume is therefore the volume of the $60\,\mathrm{HU}$ mask, not an independent physical assay of gelatin volume.

\paragraph{SPECT.}
Functional volumes were thresholded at $8\%$ of the global maximum, then refined at $40\%$ of each segment's peak voxel value.
These thresholds are applied to the PVE-affected reconstructed image; subsequent SPECT features are computed inside the resulting masks.

\paragraph{Merged-blob split.}
If a refined SPECT segment's volume was $<60\%$ of its initial estimate, the algorithm searched for a secondary local maximum ($>70\%$ of the primary peak) and reapplied the refinement.
The rule is a heuristic for splitting two inserts that have merged on SPECT.

\paragraph{One-to-one matching.}
A pair was retained only when exactly one CT segment corresponded to exactly one SPECT segment.
Many-to-one assignments (for example three CT inserts mapping to one SPECT blob) and the reverse were excluded.
Overlapping or clustered inserts are therefore absent from the dataset, and spill-in from a neighboring insert is not studied.

\subsection{Dataset composition}

Six sessions produced $75$ SPECT/CT scans of $21$ containers and $2{,}302$ validated one-to-one pairs (\Cref{tab:acquisition,tab:sessions}).
Training used sessions~$1$--$5$ ($1{,}968$ pairs).
The held-out session is a separate preparation ($334$ pairs, $13$ scans, $4$ containers), not a random split of the pooled data.
Its activity range lies inside the global range but is a different mix, including two disjoint sub-ranges; the split is a stress test of a new phantom session, not a guarantee against training-distribution bias.

\subsection{Radiomics and machine learning architecture}

The dataset of $2{,}302$ validated segments was used to train predictive models for PVE correction.
The architecture was structured as follows.

\paragraph{Feature extraction and selection.}
High-dimensional radiomic features were extracted from the SPECT data using a fixed bin width of $2{,}000$ counts.
This standardization ensured a constant intensity resolution across varying activity levels and scan sessions.
To prevent overfitting and reduce collinearity, features with near-zero variance and those exhibiting high mutual correlation were excluded.

\paragraph{Predictive modeling strategy.}
We implemented two distinct algorithmic approaches based on the target variable:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Activity density estimation.}
A random forest (RF) model was selected to capture the complex, non-linear relationship between textural radiomic features and PVE distortions.
To further enhance robustness, a hybrid model (LR$+$RF) was developed, where a linear regression (LR) baseline was refined by an RF-based error correction.

\item \textbf{Volume quantification.}
Elastic net (EN) was utilized as a regularized linear model.
While RF showed high accuracy in internal testing, EN was prioritized for its superior stability in recovering morphological volume across different phantom geometries.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Clinical simulation and validation (hold-out).}
To ensure the model's generalizability to real-world clinical data, two validation protocols were employed:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Standard randomized split.}
A conventional $80/20$ train--test split to measure internal consistency.

\item \textbf{Phantom-based ``new patient'' simulation.}
The model was tested on an entirely unseen phantom session.
Given the stochastic nature of each phantom (e.g., variations in segment clustering and manual agitation patterns), this protocol validates the system's ability to generalize to novel spatial distributions rather than memorizing session-specific artifacts.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Benchmark comparison (classic RC model).}
As a clinical baseline, a standard recovery coefficient (RC) curve was established using a NEMA image quality (IQ) phantom ($152\,\mathrm{kBq/ml}$ spheres, zero background).
Following the methodology in MIRD Pamphlet No.~32~\citep{mird32}, the RC data were fitted to a sigmoidal empirical model
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rc}
\mathrm{RC}(V) = 1 - \frac{1}{1 + (V/a)^{b}},
\end{equation}
where $V$ is the volume and $a,b$ are fitted parameters.
This ``idealized'' curve was compared against the ML models to demonstrate the improvement in quantitative accuracy, particularly for small-volume and irregularly shaped clinical targets.

\section{Results}

Segments that lacked a unique CT--SPECT correspondence (one CT segment matched to more than one SPECT segment, or vice versa) were filtered out of the dataset.
Features with low variance and features with high mutual correlation were then removed.
A random forest was trained to estimate segment activity density and segment volume, and evaluated on the held-out test data.

On a randomized train--test split, the activity-density RF model attained $R^2=0.98$ and $\mathrm{RMSE}=17.8\,\mathrm{kBq/ml}$.
To probe constancy under a distribution shift, the same model was evaluated on a new phantom rather than a random split of the pooled dataset.
In this case $R^2$ fell to $0.86$ with $\mathrm{RMSE}=31.5\,\mathrm{kBq/ml}$.
To improve these results, a linear regression on mean intensity counts was used as a first-stage predictor and the RF was trained to correct the residual.
The combined model improved the new-phantom scores to $R^2=0.89$ and $\mathrm{RMSE}=27.8\,\mathrm{kBq/ml}$.

For volume, an elastic-net model was compared with RF under both the random split and the new-phantom protocol.
Although the random-split $R^2$ of EN is only slightly higher than its new-phantom $R^2$, the difference is insignificant, indicating that the volume mapping is stable across phantom geometries.
RF, while stronger than EN on the random split, performed poorly on the new phantom, leading to the conclusion that RF does not generalize for volume prediction.

Relative to a classic recovery-coefficient curve fitted on an image-quality phantom, the RF activity-density model is substantially more accurate at small volumes, where the spherical RC model fails.
A residual train--test discrepancy remains for the RF model, but the difference is insignificant relative to the RC baseline.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This work tests whether a gelatin insert library can support data-driven PVE correction when lesions are irregular rather than spherical.
Several limits of the present experiment follow directly from that design.

The background is cold.
Spill-out from the insert is the dominant PVE mechanism; spill-in from warm surrounding tissue, which is common clinically, is not measured.

Iodinated contrast is present only in the inserts.
The gelatin base is shared, but the iodine raises insert attenuation relative to background.
Because the same CT is used for attenuation correction and for the $60\,\mathrm{HU}$ mask, that difference can bias reconstructed SPECT activity.
We did not quantify that bias.

CT volume is the volume of a $60\,\mathrm{HU}$ mask chosen in a small-scale experiment, not a separately assayed physical volume.
SPECT masks are thresholded on the already blurred emission image.

Only one-to-one CT--SPECT pairs are retained.
Adjacent or overlapping inserts---where neighbor spill-in would matter---are excluded.

Activity density is a preparation target of the labeled gelatin in each session; a dose-calibrator protocol is not reported here.
Shaking resamples pose and does not multiply the number of independent physical inserts.

The demonstration uses $^{99m}\mathrm{Tc}$.
The phantom construction is transferable in spirit to $^{177}\mathrm{Lu}$ or to PET tracers; those physics (energy, collimator, scatter, coincidence) are not shown.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presented a method for estimating the activity density and the volume of a lesion using machine-learning algorithms on radiomic properties of $2{,}302$ lesions which, unlike previous methods, include irregular shapes rather than spheres.
For activity-density estimation, the best results were obtained by linear regression on the SPECT intensity measurement with random-forest residual correction, with $R^2=0.89$ on a new phantom.
For volume estimation, $R^2=0.76$ was obtained with an elastic-net model.
